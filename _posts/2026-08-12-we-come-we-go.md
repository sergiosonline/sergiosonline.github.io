We arrived late at night. All the way there we wondered where we may end up. It's hard to avoid getting your hopes up when all you've ever known is pain. Violence, poverty, disease, rejection, we tasted them since we first opened our eyes and cried to all the judgmental stares that solemly stared. Our mother used to tell us: "We're unlocky to be alive, unluckier to have been born." But today's different. Today we begin a new chapter.

My brother and I brought all the paperwork our small frames could carry. The journey had been long and hard, and we knew this was the last leg. That big bus had picked us up near the train station, and, as quickly as we boarded, we fell asleep. I rub my eyes as I'm motioned to sit down on the ground. The lights are blinding, the concrete floor still warm from the hot, desert sun. The faces are many, of all ages, genders, colors, and shapes. Masked figures speaking a foreign tongue walk around us, staring, wielding savage weapons and dogs so large they could feed my family and I for a week.

<!-- <br> -->

—“Who do you think they are?" a woman asks me, pointing at the men with her mouth.

—“Cops. Good cops, I guess," I respond.

—“No, child. Those aren't cops. They're not even human. My uncle—"

<!-- <br> -->

One of these masked men rushes over to us, accompanied by two others. They grab the woman swiftly, with the smooth synchrony that only comes after repeating a motion over 10,000 times. The woman screams fiercely, her saliva bursting forth, punching the warm, dusty air. The three masked men are unfazed. They simply rush her out of that place. Nobody makes a peep for we were all there with no place to turn back to.

Hours go by, or so I think. Watches, cellphones, food, and water had all been taken away from us. I tell my brother that everything is gonna be ok, that we'll end up somehwere clean, warm, beautiful. He tells me it does't matter. Oh, but it does. The home we left behind does not want us. Without our mother there really isn't much left for us: Only vice and untimely death.

Maybe it's hunger, maybe exhaustion, but my brother collapses. In that moment I panic, unsure whether raising my voice would save him or condemn us both. I made my choice then. I can't say I thought about it for more than a split second. Once made though, I move swiftly. I take out our papers quietly and crumple his forms. As soon as I crumpl one, I put it in my mouth for the world to never see it again. Something tells me this is the only way.

As soon as I swallowed the last record, one of the masked men rushes to me and grabs me by the arm. He motions towards my brother, laying on the floor, a few feet apart from me now thanks to my shuffling. I keep staring at the masked figure and simply shake my head. He takes me to a place of light.

I come into the room of light looking for answers, certainty over my destiny. Instead, I leave with more questions. Why is everyone there unmasked and smiling when I enter? Why does nobody ask for any of the paperwork I brought? Why am I immediately given fresh food and clean clothes while my brother lays out there stiff and forgotten? Why is everything so quickly stamped and processed?

<p class="scene-break">.&nbsp;&nbsp;.&nbsp;&nbsp;.</p>

It's been a year today since that fateful day. Now that I have everything I know home would never have for me, I see why I made the cut. They're just like me and I'm just like them: devoid of empathy, unwilling to cede. I now dress like them and speak their language too. Deep down I know my brother deserved this life as much as I did, but it's on me to live now, and nobody, not even his dead memory, will take away what I worked so hard to get.
