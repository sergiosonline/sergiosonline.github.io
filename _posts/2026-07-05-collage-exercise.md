This work is based on James Vandermeer's exercise, as detailed in his fantastic book "Wonderbook: The Illustrated Guide to Creating Imaginative Fiction." This exercise has several steps, but I've chosen to show the first and final steps only. 

1. The first step is to establish a topic (teeth, chairs, bus stops, whatever you want). Using that topic, gather materials written by others that somehow feel like they touch on the topic. These materials can be fiction, nonfiction, poetry, etc.

2. Collage these sentences and passages together to create 2 or 3 paragraphs, keeping the original material virtually untouched.

3. Take the collage and rewrite it, keeping the original tone of the material as best as you can, but smoothing out the transitions and tone.

4. Lastly, rewrite the collage one more time to reflect your style, as well as the point of view of a character of your choosing or your creating. Change as many words as you see fit.

## The Final Piece

From the window I saw horses, our horses. I was outside of Berlin, in winter, where light has no light and the sky often lacks heaven. The air, white like wet bread, crisp with a morning dew that had quickly been turned to frost. There was a vacant area outside of my window, our window, bitten by the teeth of winter. Suddenly ten horses appeared. I recognized one and thought how the filly, my filly Kris, was never still. She had no restraint, only violence. She showed flat ears, flared nostrils, clear eyes; living, overflowing muscle taking over her rightful space wherever she was.

I’ve only come to have horses recently, but I learned the best precept early, a golden rule of sorts: Never approach them in anger. Anger will blind judgment, skew movement, burn intention. Anger leads to regret, making people do things they wouldn’t do in her absence. Seeing my filly then, I said aloud: “When she bites my hands, she bites me hard. I feel the whole thing. Every single second…” My precious Rika, keeping her eyes on the horses, told me to go on. “I’m in her mouth. She bites, and I’m gone. Suddenly gone.” Where to, asks Rika. “Nowhere. Everywhere. I’m her. She’s me.”

I’ve been trying to think of my filly and her mother. The mother stood on the road one morning, showing no movement. My precious Rika and I sat behind her for nearly an hour, my car still on to keep the heat going, the seats smelling of our fresh sex. The horse stepped away in slow motion, never looking back at us. Rika was my caretaker turned lover turned everything. I asked her then: “Can you help her?,” to which she told me she didn’t know yet. “But you’ve seen horses like this before,” I said. Rika fired back: “I’ve seen horses that were hurt, yes. She doesn’t seem hurt.” I picked up the tempo: “And?”. “And it depends on the horse. It depends on what they want,” Rika replied. I told her horses don’t want things. Rika darted her eyes to me and replied coldly: “Dearest, that’s where you’re wrong.”

I still see my filly, strong, unchecked. She was never quiet. There was something in her that had no patience with stillness, with fences, with the ordinary world. My Rika and Kris had a lot more in common than I had with either of them. They deserve each other. Alone, in this shitty room, I tell myself that stupid horse is what I would be if I could.
